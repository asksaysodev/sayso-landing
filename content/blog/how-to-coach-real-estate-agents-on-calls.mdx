To coach real estate agents on their calls, review a few real calls with each agent every week using a short scorecard, role play the exact moment that went wrong, and check the next week's calls to see whether the change stuck. Ride-alongs and live help during the call add to that routine, but the weekly review and practice loop is what lets one team leader coach a whole team.

This guide compares the four ways to coach calls (call review, role play, ride-alongs and live coaching), gives you a scorecard and a feedback script to copy, and lays out a weekly routine for team coaching for real estate agents that takes about four and a half hours a week for a team of eight. It is written for team leaders, sales managers and ISA managers. If you are choosing an outside coach for yourself, start with our guide to [real estate coaching programs](/blog/real-estate-coaching-programs/).

## Four Ways to Coach Agents on Their Calls

Each method fixes a different problem, and none of them works alone. The times below are suggestions to build a routine around, not research findings.

| Method | What it fixes | Suggested time per agent per week | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Call review** | Patterns in the opener, discovery questions and the appointment ask | 10 minutes of listening plus 20 minutes in a one-on-one | Needs recordings, notes or agent-selected moments, and feedback arrives after the call |
| **Role play** | The exact moment that went wrong, such as one objection | 10 to 15 minutes, inside the one-on-one | A practice setting has less pressure than a live call |
| **Ride-along** | Tone, pacing and listening, which show up only live | 30 to 60 minutes, one agent at a time | Does not scale, and agents may sound different when someone is listening |
| **Live coaching during the call** | The moment the agent is stuck mid-conversation | Automatic, on every call | Supplements your coaching; does not replace it |

Most team leaders do some of the first three and skip the system around them. The routine below puts them in an order that repeats every week.

## Step 1: Define What a Good Call Sounds Like

You cannot coach consistently without a shared standard. Write a short scorecard your whole team uses, so feedback is about the call and not about your mood that day. Keep it to five lines and keep it neutral about whether the lead is a buyer or a seller.

| Stage | Listen for | Yes / Partly / No |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | States who is calling and why in the first 20 seconds, and sounds like a person instead of a script | |
| Discovery | Asks at least two open questions about the prospect's situation and timing, and lets them finish | |
| Pushback | Acknowledges the concern, answers briefly, and redirects with a question instead of arguing | |
| Appointment ask | Asks for a specific next step and offers two time choices | |
| Wrap-up | Confirms the next step out loud and records the notes in the CRM | |

Adjust the lines to your market and lead sources, and share the scorecard with your agents before you use it. When agents know what you are listening for, review feels like coaching instead of an audit. For ideas on what good language sounds like, see [what top agents say on calls](/blog/what-top-agents-say-on-calls/).

## Step 2: Choose Which Calls to Review

You do not need to listen to every call. Each week, ask each agent to flag two moments: one that went well and one that was hard. Add one at random if you want a check on how the agent picks. Listen to the first minute and to the moment where the call turned, rather than the whole recording.

If you do not record calls, coach from the agent's notes, the CRM history and ride-alongs, and ask the agent to describe the hard moment in their own words.

<CalloutBox type="warning">
Check the recording rules before you record agent calls. Federal law requires the consent of at least one party to a call, and a number of states, including California, Florida, Illinois, Pennsylvania and Washington, require everyone on the call to consent. Confirm your state's rule and your brokerage's policy first. This is general information, not legal advice.
</CalloutBox>

## Step 3: Hold the Call Review

Twenty minutes is enough for one call when the agent talks first. The goal is for the agent to find the problem before you point it out, because an agent who spots the problem tends to fix it faster than one who is simply told.

1. **Ask the agent to go first.** What were they trying to get from the call, and where did it feel off?
2. **Play the moment.** Listen to the 30 seconds around the turning point together.
3. **Name one thing that worked.** Use the agent's actual words so the praise is specific.
4. **Pick one change.** One fix per session, not a list. Agents who leave with five changes tend to apply very few of them.
5. **Agree on how you will both know it worked.** For example, "next week I will listen to your first two calls and check whether you ask for two times."

<ScriptExample label="Opening the Review">
Before I tell you anything, tell me what you were trying to get from that call and where it felt off to you.
</ScriptExample>

<ScriptExample label="Giving the Feedback">
Here is what I heard. When you opened, you said [exact words that worked], and the prospect stayed with you. The moment I would change is when they said [what the prospect said]. You answered with [what the agent said], and the conversation stalled. Try [alternative line] and end with a question. Do you want to try that with me right now?
</ScriptExample>

## Step 4: Role Play the Exact Moment

Role play works best when it practices the actual line from a real call, not a generic scenario. Take the prospect's exact words from the review and play the prospect yourself.

1. Play the prospect using their real words.
2. Let the agent respond, then change one thing (the first sentence, the tone, the question at the end).
3. Repeat three times, then have the agent say the improved version aloud twice more.
4. Ask the agent to try it with a teammate before the next shift of calls.

Scenario cards keep Friday huddles organized. Use the objections your team hears most, and link each card to the matching response guide:

| Prospect says | What the agent practices | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| "We already have an agent." | Staying curious without criticizing the other agent | [Already have an agent](/objections/already-have-an-agent/) |
| "Call me later." | Getting a specific time and a reason to speak now | [Call me later](/objections/call-me-later/) |
| "We are not ready yet." | Learning the real timeline and offering a low-pressure next step | [Not ready yet](/objections/not-ready-yet/) |
| "What is your commission?" | Answering plainly and returning to value | [How much is your commission](/objections/how-much-is-your-commission/) |

Our guides to [real estate role play](/blog/real-estate-role-play/) and [practicing real estate scripts](/blog/how-to-practice-real-estate-scripts/) go deeper on how agents can practice between sessions.

## Step 5: Ride Along Without Taking Over

Listening to a live call is the fastest way to hear tone and pacing, which are hard to judge from a note. Sit in with the scorecard in hand, stay silent unless the call is going badly, and debrief the same day while the agent remembers the conversation.

Ride-alongs are most valuable for new agents in their first weeks, for diagnosing an agent whose numbers dropped, and for ISAs whose volume makes small habits expensive. They are a poor routine for a whole team, since you can only listen to one agent at a time and the other agents are on their own meanwhile.

## Team Coaching for Real Estate Agents: A Weekly Routine

Here is a routine for a team of eight that you can copy and adjust.

| Day | Who | What | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Each agent | Flags two moments (one good, one hard) in a shared sheet | 5 minutes each |
| Tuesday and Wednesday | You | Listen to the flagged moments and fill in the scorecard | 10 minutes per agent |
| Wednesday and Thursday | You and each agent | One-on-one: review (step 3) and role play (step 4) | 20 minutes per agent |
| Friday | The whole team | Huddle: one role play on the week's most common objection, plus one call that went well | 30 minutes |
| Next Monday | You | Check whether each agent used the new line in the first two calls of the week | Part of the next listening block |

The last row is the one most teams skip. Coaching without a follow-up check turns into advice that is easy to forget.

### How Many Hours Does It Take?

Using 30 minutes per agent a week (10 minutes of listening plus the 20-minute one-on-one) and a 30-minute Friday huddle, the weekly time grows with the team:

| Team size | Everyone weekly | Tiered (see below) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 agents | 2.5 hours | Same |
| 8 agents | 4.5 hours | Same |
| 12 agents | 6.5 hours | about 4.1 hours |

Beyond about eight agents, weekly one-on-ones with everyone start to crowd out your own production. Tier the cadence instead:

- **Newer agents and anyone whose numbers dropped:** weekly one-on-one.
- **Steady agents:** every other week.
- **Top producers:** monthly, and ask them to share one call that went well at the Friday huddle.

A team of 12 with 4 agents weekly, 5 every other week and 3 monthly comes to about 3.6 hours of one-on-one time (120 + 75 + 22.5 minutes) plus the 30-minute huddle.

## Common Mistakes When Coaching Calls

- **Coaching the numbers instead of the conversation.** Conversion rates tell you which agent needs help, but only the call tells you what to say differently.
- **Giving five fixes at once.** One change per session is enough, and an agent who masters it comes back ready for the next.
- **Only coaching the weakest agents.** Steady agents often lose appointments in small habits that no dashboard shows, so keep them on a lighter cycle.
- **Correcting agents in front of the team.** Praise in the huddle and correct in the one-on-one.
- **Skipping the follow-up.** Check the next week's calls, or the review has no effect.
- **Using one script for every lead.** Buyer and seller conversations differ, so coach each call against its own goal and use [objection responses](/blog/real-estate-objection-handling/) that fit the lead type.

## FAQ

**How often should I coach each agent on their calls?**
Weekly works for newer agents and anyone whose numbers dropped, every other week for steady agents, and monthly for top producers. Adjust as you see results.

**What if an agent resists coaching?**
Ask them to choose the calls, start with something they did well, and limit each session to one change tied to a goal they set. Agents usually resist feedback that feels like a verdict, and respond when it feels like help reaching their own numbers.

**Do I have to record calls to coach them?**
No. You can coach from agent-selected moments, CRM notes and ride-alongs. If you do record, confirm the consent rules first.

**How do I coach an ISA?**
ISAs make many calls a day, so listen to short clips instead of whole calls and focus on one habit at a time, such as the opening or the appointment ask.
