To book appointments in real estate, treat every lead call as a series of five decisions: re-anchor the lead to why you are calling, find the reason behind their inquiry, decide whether a meeting would do something the call cannot, ask for it with two specific times, and handle the pushback with a question before you answer it. Agents who book consistently are not using a better closing line. They make those five decisions in order, on purpose, on every call.

This guide walks through that call flow from the first 20 seconds to the confirmation text, with the scripts our agents use at each step and annotated exchanges that show what the agent says, how the prospect usually responds, and what the agent does next and why. It is the hub of our appointment-setting series, so where a single step has its own deep guide, we link to it instead of repeating it here.

## Where These Scripts Come From

The scripts in this guide come from the objection book agents use at Anderson Real Estate Group (AREG), the team where Kuvaal Patel runs agent development. They are short on purpose. Each response is built to be read in a few seconds while the prospect is still talking, and nearly every one follows the same shape: acknowledge what the prospect said, ask one question, and come back to a time.

The call examples are illustrative. They show how these exchanges typically go on seller and buyer lead calls, not a transcript of any specific call or person.

## How to Book Appointments in Real Estate: Five Decision Points

In call review, the ask is rarely where the call broke. It usually broke a minute or two earlier, at a decision the agent did not know they were making. Here is the whole flow on one page.

| Stage | The decision you are making | What you listen for | What you do next | Go deeper |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Re-anchor | Does this person know why I am calling, and are they still the right person? | Confusion, "who is this," "I never signed up" | State the source, date and address or search, then confirm it is still theirs | [How to start a real estate call](/blog/how-to-start-a-real-estate-call/) |
| 2. Find the reason | Is there a move behind this inquiry, or only curiosity? | Timeline words, life events, "we" | Ask what they would do with the answer they were looking for | [Questions to ask real estate leads](/blog/questions-to-ask-real-estate-leads/) |
| 3. Earn the meeting | Is there something a meeting does that this call cannot? | Condition, upgrades, a specific home, two decision makers, two moves to time | Name what the meeting is for in one sentence | This guide, below |
| 4. Ask | Am I offering two real times, or a yes-or-no question? | Your own wording | "Today at [Time] or tomorrow at [Time]?" | [Appointment setting script](/blog/appointment-setting-script/) |
| 5. Handle pushback | What is the concern under the objection? | The first reason they give | Acknowledge, ask one question, return to a time or a dated callback | [Think about it objection](/blog/think-about-it-objection/) |

Two rules sit across all five stages. Never skip a stage to reach the ask faster, because a skipped stage comes back later as an objection. And every call ends with either the appointment or a dated callback on the calendar.

## Decision 1: Re-Anchor the Lead in the First 20 Seconds

Online leads forget. Someone checked a home value or saved a search three weeks ago, and now a stranger is calling them. The first decision is whether the prospect knows why you are calling. If they do not, your only job is to fix that, calmly and with specifics.

These are the openers our agents use when the lead does not remember. The seller version:

<ScriptExample label="Seller Lead: I Never Requested a Home Value Report">
Totally fair, a lot of people check their home value online and forget about it. It came in for [Address] on [Date]. Is that still your home? Were you curious about the value, or did something get you thinking about selling?
</ScriptExample>

The buyer version:

<ScriptExample label="Buyer Lead: I Never Signed Up for Anything">
No problem. It came in through [Lead Source] on [Date], looking at homes in [City]. A lot of people save a search and forget they registered. Are you still looking around [City], or was that just browsing?
</ScriptExample>

And when the prospect asks how you got their number:

<ScriptExample label="How Did You Get My Number">
Fair question. Your info came in through [Lead Source] on [Date] for [Address]. I'm [Your Name] with [Company Name], we help homeowners in [City]. Are you still at [Address]?
</ScriptExample>

All three agree first, give three concrete facts (source, date, address or area), and end with an easy question. A specific, verifiable answer lowers the prospect's guard faster than any rapport line.

Here is how that exchange usually goes on a seller lead.

**Agent:** "Hi, is this Dana? This is Chris with [Company Name]. I'm following up on the home value request for the house on Maple."

**Prospect:** "I don't remember asking for anything."

**Agent:** "No problem, it came through [Lead Source] on the 12th. Most people forget, it only takes a click. Is Maple still your home?"

**Prospect:** "Yeah, we've been here about eight years."

**What the agent does next and why:** The agent does not apologize again or explain the company. The prospect just confirmed ownership and volunteered a detail (eight years), which means the guard is down. The agent moves straight to Decision 2: "If the value came back different than you expected, would that change anything for you?" That asks about plans without asking "are you selling," which most prospects deflect.

One hard stop belongs in this stage. If the prospect says "take me off your list," the team script is a single sentence: "Understood, I'll remove you right now. You won't hear from me again." Then do it. There is no rebuttal for that request, and your compliance obligations come first. Our guide to [do not call rules for real estate agents](/blog/do-not-call-rules-real-estate-agents/) covers the details.

## Decision 2: Find the Reason Behind the Inquiry

Nobody checks their home value or saves a search for no reason. The second decision is whether there is a move behind the inquiry or only curiosity, and you make it by asking what the prospect would do with the answer they went looking for.

The reasons are usually personal. In the [2024 NAR Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers](https://nar.realtor/sites/default/files/2024-11/2024-profile-of-home-buyers-and-sellers-highlights-11-04-2024_2.pdf), the most common reason sellers gave for selling was wanting to move closer to friends and family, at 23 percent. A question about what is changing in their life will usually tell you more than a question about price.

<ScriptExample label="Seller Lead: I Was Just Curious What My Home Is Worth">
Makes sense. Curiosity usually has a reason behind it. If the value came in at [Price], what would you do with that? Is it more someday-move curious, or just keeping tabs?
</ScriptExample>

<ScriptExample label="Buyer Lead: I'm Just Browsing Online">
Perfect, that's the best time to start. How long have you been looking at homes? What's got you browsing? If you found the right home tomorrow, would you be ready to move on it?
</ScriptExample>

The "someday-move curious, or just keeping tabs" line works because it gives the prospect two acceptable options and makes neither one embarrassing. The buyer line "if you found the right home tomorrow" lets the prospect picture the move without committing to anything.

Here is how that exchange usually goes on a buyer lead.

**Agent:** "What's got you browsing?"

**Prospect:** "Our lease is up in the spring and the rent keeps going up. We're just seeing what's out there."

**Agent:** "When is the lease up?"

**Prospect:** "End of April."

**What the agent does next and why:** The prospect gave a reason (rent), a date (April), and said "we." That is enough to move to Decision 3. Instead of five more qualifying questions, the agent asks one that sets up the meeting: "Have you talked with a lender yet, or would it help to know your numbers before you start touring?" That question tests whether the meeting should be a lender conversation, a buyer consultation, or a showing.

If the prospect gives you nothing ("just keeping tabs, no plans"), that is still a decision: this lead is not a meeting today, so book a dated callback instead of forcing an ask. ## Decision 3: Decide Whether a Meeting Is Worth Booking

This is the decision most agents skip. Before you ask for a meeting, you should be able to finish this sentence out loud: "The meeting is worth your time because ___." If you cannot, the prospect cannot either, and you will hear "just send it to me."

The meeting has to do something the call cannot. In our scripts, it is always one of a short list of things.

| What the prospect told you | What the meeting does that the call cannot | The line from the team scripts |
|---|---|---|
| They want an exact home value | Sees condition and upgrades that online values miss | "Online values can't see condition or upgrades." |
| They cite an online estimate that is higher | Walks the home and compares it with recent sales on their street | "Would you like a value based on what buyers paid on [Street Name] in the last [Timeframe]?" |
| They asked about one specific listing | Gets them inside the home instead of judging photos | "Have you been inside it yet, or just seen the photos?" |
| They need to sell before they buy, or buy before they sell | Lays out the ways to time both moves | "There are a few ways to time both moves. Want to walk through them on [Day] at [Time]?" |
| Someone else is part of the decision | Puts both decision makers in the same conversation | "Let's set a time when you can both be on." |
| They are unsure they have enough equity | Turns a guess into a starting number | "Let's find out for sure." |

Some situations do not justify a meeting with you yet, and the scripts say so. When a buyer says their credit is not good enough or they do not have a down payment, the team script points them to a lender first ("A lender is the right person to answer that. I can share a few you can choose from.") and offers to send listings in the meantime.

Here is how the "just email me" version of this decision usually goes on a seller lead.

**Agent:** "I can put together a value based on what sold nearby."

**Prospect:** "Just email it to me."

**Agent:** "Happy to. Online values can't see condition or upgrades. What have you updated since you bought it?"

**Prospect:** "We redid the kitchen two years ago and put in a new roof."

**What the agent does next and why:** The prospect just gave the agent the reason for the meeting. A remodeled kitchen and a new roof are things a computer estimate cannot price. The agent ties the ask directly to it: "I'll email it now. Then 15 minutes at the house to fine tune it for the kitchen and the roof. Today at 5 or tomorrow at 10?" The prospect gets the email, and the meeting has a purpose they named themselves.

That is the pattern to train: agree to the request, ask one question that surfaces something the call cannot handle, then name the meeting as the place where it gets handled. ## Decision 4: Make the Ask With Two Specific Times

The same ask repeats across the team scripts: "Would today at [Time] or tomorrow at [Time] work?" That consistency is deliberate. When an agent has to invent the ask on each call, it comes out as "would you maybe want to set something up at some point?" and the prospect says no because no is easier.

Three details make the standard ask work.

**It names the length.** "A full home value takes about 15 minutes at the house" or "20 minutes on [Day]" tells the prospect what they are agreeing to. An open-ended meeting sounds like a sales presentation. A 15-minute one sounds manageable.

**It offers two real times close together.** Today and tomorrow, or two slots in the next 48 hours. The closer the meeting is to the call, the less time there is for plans to change. When a prospect has an urgent reason to meet and you can clear the time, [same-day appointments](/blog/how-to-get-same-day-appointments/) are worth offering.

**It comes right after the prospect's reason.** The ask lands best in the sentence after the prospect tells you something the meeting will solve, as in the kitchen and roof example above.

## Decision 5: Handle the Pushback With a Question First

Almost every first ask gets some pushback. The decision at this stage is not "what is my rebuttal." It is "what is the concern underneath what they just said." The team scripts answer that the same way every time: acknowledge, ask one question, then come back to a time.

<ScriptExample label="Seller: We Want to Wait Until Spring">
Totally fine. What's driving the spring timeline, school calendar, weather, or something else? Would it help to have a plan in place now so you're ready when spring comes? Can we sit down for 20 minutes on [Day] at [Time]?
</ScriptExample>

<ScriptExample label="Buyer: I Only Wanted Info on This One Home">
Got it. What stood out about [Property Address]? Have you been inside it yet, or just seen the photos? Would you like to see it in person today at [Time] or tomorrow at [Time]?
</ScriptExample>

<ScriptExample label="Seller or Buyer: I Need to Talk to Someone Else First">
Of course. Who else is part of the decision? What do you think [Decision Maker] will ask first? Let's set a time when you can both be on. Would today at [Time] or tomorrow at [Time] work?
</ScriptExample>

The question in the middle is what does the work. "What's driving the spring timeline" tells you whether spring is a school calendar (a fixed date you can plan backward from) or a vague hope that the market improves (a different conversation).

Here is how the "someone else" exchange usually goes.

**Agent:** "Would today at 6 or tomorrow at 10 work to walk through the numbers?"

**Prospect:** "I'd have to talk to my husband first."

**Agent:** "Of course. What do you think he'll ask first?"

**Prospect:** "Probably whether we can afford to buy before we sell. He's worried about carrying two mortgages."

**What the agent does next and why:** The objection was never "my husband." It was the fear of carrying two payments, and the prospect said it out loud because the agent asked what the other person would ask. The agent now has a reason for the meeting that both spouses care about: "That's the right question. There are a few ways to time both moves so you're not carrying two payments for long. Let's set a time when you can both be on. Would tonight at 7 or tomorrow at 6 work?" The agent never argued or repeated the original ask.

## When the Answer Is Not Yet, Book the Next Call

Some prospects should not get a meeting today. They have no timeline, no reason, and no decision to make for months. Forcing the ask on them burns the relationship. The decision here is to stop asking for the appointment and start asking for the next conversation, with a date on it.

The team scripts for these situations all end the same way, with a specific date.

<ScriptExample label="FSBO: We'll Call You If It Doesn't Sell">
Fair enough. What date would you give yourself before calling an agent? Can I check in on [Date] to see how it's going? In the meantime, want a list of what sold nearby?
</ScriptExample>

<ScriptExample label="Buyer: Our Lease Isn't Up Until Later">
When is the lease up, [Date]? When would you want to start looking so the timing lines up? Can I send you listings in the meantime so you get a feel for the market?
</ScriptExample>

The FSBO line "what date would you give yourself" gets the prospect to name their own deadline, so the follow-up call on that date is one they agreed to.

Here is how that usually goes.

**Agent:** "What date would you give yourself before calling an agent?"

**Prospect:** "If it hasn't sold in a month, I guess we'd think about it."

**Agent:** "That's fair. Can I check in on the 15th to see how it's going?"

**Prospect:** "Sure."

**What the agent does next and why:** The agent puts a callback for the 15th in the CRM with the note "FSBO, gave themselves one month, open to agent after." On the 15th, the call opens with the prospect's own words: "You mentioned giving it a month before talking to an agent. How has it gone?" That is a different call from "just checking in."

## Lock the Appointment Before You Hang Up

A booked appointment is only half done. Between now and the meeting the prospect will get busy, get another agent's call, or forget why they said yes. Confirmation is a separate skill from booking, and it starts before you hang up.

There is little published data on no-show rates for real estate appointments, but healthcare has studied the problem closely. A [systematic review of appointment no-shows](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29482948/) published in Health Policy in 2018 put the average no-show rate at about 23 percent. A doctor's visit is not a listing appointment, but the lesson carries over: a meaningful share of booked meetings will not happen unless someone works to keep them on the calendar.

Our confirmation sequence has four touches.

1. **Recap on the call.** Before you hang up, restate the time, place and purpose, and ask what else they want you to bring. "So I have you down for Wednesday at 4 at the house on Maple. I'll bring the sales from your street and we'll account for the kitchen and the roof. Anything else you want me to come ready with?" The prospect hears the reason for the meeting one more time, in their own words.
2. **Text within 30 minutes.** Reference one detail they gave you. "Great talking with you, Dana. See you Wednesday at 4. I'm pulling the two sales on Maple from this spring."
3. **Day-before confirmation.** A short text, voice message or video from you, confirming the time and saying what you prepared.
4. **Morning-of logistics text.** "Looking forward to 4 o'clock. Is the side door or the front door easier?" Ask about logistics, never "are we still on?" That question invites a reschedule the prospect had not thought about.

If both decision makers are not confirmed for the meeting, fix that before the day-before touch. A listing appointment with one spouse missing often turns into a second appointment.

## How We Coach This Call Flow on a Team

When an agent's appointments are down, the instinct is to drill closing lines. We score reviewed conversations against the five decisions instead, because the miss usually shows up earlier in the call.

| Checkpoint | What we look for in review | The most common miss |
|---|---|---|
| Re-anchor | Source, date and address or area stated in the first 20 seconds | Opening with the agent's name and company and nothing the prospect recognizes |
| Reason | At least one question about what they would do with the answer | Jumping from "is this still your home" straight to the ask |
| Meeting purpose | The agent names what the meeting does that the call cannot | "I'd love to come by and chat" with no reason attached |
| Ask | Two specific times, with a stated length | "When would be a good time for you?" |
| Pushback | One question before any rebuttal | Answering the stated objection instead of the concern under it |
| Next step | Appointment or dated callback in the CRM | "I'll follow up with you" with no date |

Scoring this way tells the agent exactly which decision to fix, instead of a general note to "be more confident," and shows a team leader whether a slump is a skill problem or a lead problem. If agents are making all six checkpoints and appointments are still down, look at the leads before you look at the agents.

## FAQ

**How do I book appointments in real estate consistently?**

Make the same five decisions on every call: re-anchor the lead, find the reason behind their inquiry, decide what the meeting does that the call cannot, ask with two specific times, and ask a question before you answer an objection. Then end every call with either an appointment or a dated callback.

**What should I say when a lead says they never asked to be contacted?**

Agree, then give specifics: the lead source, the date, and the address or area they searched, followed by a simple question such as "Is that still your home?" or "Are you still looking around [City]?" Specific, verifiable details lower the prospect's guard faster than an apology or a pitch. If they ask to be removed from your list, remove them and end the call.

**How long should I say the appointment will take?**

Name a short, specific length, such as 15 minutes for a home value walk-through or 20 minutes for a planning conversation. A stated length tells the prospect exactly what they are agreeing to. Once you are sitting together, the meeting can last as long as the prospect wants.

**What if the prospect is not ready for a meeting at all?**

Stop asking for the appointment and ask for the next conversation instead, with a date on it. Questions like "What date would you give yourself before calling an agent?" or "When would you want to start looking so the timing lines up?" let the prospect set their own follow-up date, which makes the next call one they agreed to.
