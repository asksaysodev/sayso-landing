Buyer consultation questions fall into two stages. On the first call, you ask a handful of questions to understand the buyer's motivation, timeline, and financing, and to book the consultation. At the consultation meeting, you go deeper: what the next home has to do for them, how they will pay for it, who makes the decision, and how you will work together.

Most agents mix the two up. They ask everything on the first call and have nothing left to cover at the meeting, or they skip the meeting and start touring homes with a buyer they barely know. The consultation is where a lead becomes a client with a plan, so the questions deserve their own structure. This guide sits inside our broader playbook on [how to talk to real estate leads](/blog/how-to-talk-to-real-estate-leads/) and covers the buyer qualification questions for both stages.

<ScriptExample label="Opening the Buyer Consultation">
Thanks for sitting down with me. My goal today is simple: by the end of this, you'll know exactly how buying a home works from here, and I'll know exactly what you need, so every home we look at is worth your time.

Let's start with the big picture. When you picture yourself in the next home, what's different about your life compared to today?
</ScriptExample>

- **If they say** "We just want to start seeing houses": say "We will, and soon. Give me 20 minutes so the homes we see actually fit. What's the one thing the next home has to fix?"
- **If they say** "More space, mostly": say "What does more space make possible for you? An office, a room for a new baby, room for family to visit?"
- **If they say** "We're not sure we're ready to buy": say "That's a good thing to figure out today. What would need to be true for you to feel ready?"
- **If they say** "We've already been looking with another agent": say "Good to know. Did you sign anything with them, or were you mostly touring on your own?"
- **If they say** "We already know what we want": say "Great, that makes this faster. Walk me through it, and I'll ask about anything that could change the search."

## Buyer Consultation Questions by Stage

This table shows what to ask at each stage and what each answer tells you. Write the answers down in the buyer's words, because you will use them in every follow-up.

| First call | Are you working with an agent yet? | Representation status | Agent name, signed or not |

## Stage 1: Questions for the First Call

The first call has one job: understand enough to book the consultation. Keep it short. Four or five questions are usually enough, and the [LPMAMA framework](/blog/lpmama-real-estate/) gives you the structure for them: location, price, motivation, agent, mortgage, and the appointment itself.

The questions that matter most on the first call are motivation and timeline, because together they tell you whether the buyer is serious. A buyer with a clear reason and a clear date is ready for a consultation. A buyer with neither needs a different follow-up plan, and our guide on [how to qualify real estate leads](/blog/how-to-qualify-real-estate-leads/) covers how to grade them.

When the buyer asks you to send listings, acknowledge it and bring them back to the plan:

<ScriptExample label="Redirecting to the Consultation">
I'm happy to send homes. So I send the right ones, let's take 20 minutes and map out exactly what you're looking for. Would later today or tomorrow be easier?
</ScriptExample>

- **If they say** "Can't you just set up a search for me?": say "I can, but an automated search sends everything that matches a price and a bedroom count. Twenty minutes together means you only see homes worth your time." Our page on [just send listings](/objections/just-send-listings/) has more ways to handle this.
- **If they say** "We're just browsing for now": say "That makes sense. What would have to happen for browsing to turn into buying?" See the full [just looking](/objections/just-looking/) response.

## Stage 2: Questions for the Consultation Meeting

At the meeting, you have time to go deeper. Group your questions into five areas so the conversation flows instead of feeling like an intake form.

### Goals and motivation

Start here, because everything else depends on it. The first answer is usually the surface reason, so keep going until you hear why it matters and what happens if nothing changes.

- When you picture the next home, what's different about your life compared to today?
- What's frustrating about where you live now?
- What would owning this home change for you personally?
- What happens if you don't find something in the next few months?

### Must-haves and limits

- What are the three things the next home has to have?
- What would be nice but isn't required?
- What would make you walk away from a home, even if everything else was right?
- If the perfect home came up just outside your area, would you consider it?
- How important are commute, schools, and being close to family?

### Timeline and current situation

- When does your lease end, or do you own your current home?
- Do you need to sell before you can buy?
- Is there a date you need to be moved in by, like a new job or the start of a school year?

### Decision makers

- Who else is part of this decision?
- Is anyone helping with the down payment who will want a say?
- When you've made big decisions before, how did you decide?

### Working together

- How do you like to communicate, and what times work best?
- What did you like or not like about working with agents in the past?
- How will you know you've found the right home?

<CalloutBox type="tip">
Ask the decision makers question early in the meeting. If a parent is helping with the down payment and will want to see every home, you need to know that before the first tour, not after the first offer.
</CalloutBox>

## How to Ask About Financing Without Sounding Pushy

Buyers can feel judged when you ask about money. Frame the question as protecting them, not screening them. This lender conversation works whether or not they already have one.

<ScriptExample label="The Lender Question">
Have you had the chance to talk with a lender yet, or is that something I can help with?
</ScriptExample>

- **If they say** "Not yet": say "Got it. The biggest mistake I see buyers make is waiting until they find the perfect home to figure out financing. That's how people overpay or lose the house. Would it help if I connected you with a lender we trust?"
- **If they say** "Yes, we're already pre-approved": say "That's great. The only way to know you're getting the best terms is to have a second opinion. You don't have to switch, but would you be open to a quick comparison so you can take it back to your lender?"
- **If they say** "We're paying cash": say "That's a strong position. Would you be comfortable sharing proof of funds before we write an offer, so sellers take you seriously right away?"

Follow the lender question with a budget question in monthly terms: "What monthly payment feels comfortable, all in?" Buyers think about their monthly budget more than list price, and the gap between the two is where many searches go wrong.

## How to Ask About Other Agents

Ask plainly, without making the buyer feel caught. The goal is to know where you stand and to respect any agreement they already have.

<ScriptExample label="The Agent Question">
Before we go further, are you working with any other agents right now, or have you signed anything with anyone?
</ScriptExample>

- **If they say** "We talked to one but didn't sign anything": say "Thanks for telling me. What made you want to keep looking?"
- **If they say** "Yes, we signed with someone": say "Then you're in good hands, and I don't want to get in the middle of that. If anything changes, I'm happy to help." For the full script, see [already have an agent](/objections/already-have-an-agent/).

End the consultation with the buyer agreement. On the listing side, nobody considers a seller a client until there is a signed listing agreement, and the same standard should apply to buyers. Explain what the agreement covers, how you are paid, and what they can expect from you, then ask them to sign before the first tour.

## FAQ

**What questions should I ask at a buyer consultation?**
Ask about the buyer's goals and motivation, must-haves and walk-away items, timeline and whether they need to sell first, who else is part of the decision, financing and monthly budget, other agents, and how they like to communicate. Finish by reviewing the buyer agreement.

**What is the difference between buyer consultation questions and LPMAMA?**
LPMAMA is the short qualifying framework for the first call. The buyer consultation goes deeper at the meeting you booked on that call, covering priorities, decision makers, contingencies, and how you will work together. See our [LPMAMA guide](/blog/lpmama-real-estate/) for the first-call version.

**How long should a buyer consultation take?**
Most take 30 to 60 minutes. That is enough time to understand the buyer's situation, explain the process, review the buyer agreement, and set the first tour.

**How do I ask a buyer about money without making it awkward?**
Frame financing as protecting them. Ask whether they have talked with a lender, explain that sorting out financing early keeps them from overpaying or losing a home, and offer a lender introduction. Ask about budget in monthly terms rather than total price.

**Should the buyer consultation be in person?**
In person builds the most trust, but a video meeting works well when schedules are tight. The questions are the same either way, and what matters most is booking it soon, ideally today or tomorrow.

**What should I do if the buyer will not commit to a consultation?**
Find out what is holding them back, and set a specific next step instead of sending listings and hoping. Buyers who cannot name a reason or a timeline usually belong in a longer follow-up plan through [lead nurturing](/glossary/lead-nurturing/).
